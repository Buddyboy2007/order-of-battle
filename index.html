The whole board, at once

Every sovereign state on Earth is on the map — all 198 of them — and left to it. No one is playing: each nation is run by the same engine, picking its moment, its neighbour and its service. You pick a flag and watch what happens to it.

A turn is a month, and wars are not settled in one. A front opens, both sides burn materiel on it for years, and the line moves a province at a time until somebody runs out of country. Strength comes from the ground itself — an army is only ever as large as the territory feeding it — so taking land grows you and losing it bleeds you.

How a war runs

  1. Neighbours only. A state may only declare on a country it shares a border with. A state with no foreign border left — an island, or one that has swallowed everyone around it — puts troops on ships instead, and only then.
  2. Doctrine. Each side fights the way its own numbers say it fights. The United States comes out air-led with everything behind it; Chile comes out naval-led; Switzerland is ground and nothing else. None of it is hand-written — it is read off the pillar scores and the hardware each country keeps per soldier.
  3. The line. Every month the front shifts a little toward whoever is winning the exchange, and both sides lose materiel doing it. A province changes hands when the line finally breaks.
  4. Orders. Every unit is assigned to defend, invade or patrol. Defenders hold their post, invaders push toward an active front, and patrols roam until they detect a nearby enemy and hunt it.
  5. Duration. Wars do not expire into ceasefires or draws. A front keeps grinding until a country falls, and the simulation stops only when one country remains.
  6. Last resort. A nuclear state facing annihilation may not go quietly.

About the numbers. Every figure on the cards and on the map — personnel, ground vehicles, tanks, naval vessels, aircraft, warheads, budget, and the six ATLAS pillar scores — is taken from GlobalMilitary.net's 2026 ATLAS and branch inventory tables, updated between 18 and 27 October 2026. On the situation map, one ship symbol is one naval vessel; ground and air hardware is grouped into readable formations whose printed labels show how many real vehicles or aircraft they represent. Those inventory counts determine the mass committed to each front and the equipment lost there; the branch scores continue to represent quality, composition and availability. The engagement simulation remains a game mechanic with deliberate randomness, not a forecast. Real conflicts turn on terrain, alliances, logistics, intelligence and political will that no index captures. The world map uses Natural Earth 110m boundaries on an Equal Earth projection; adjacency is derived from those boundaries, with sea crossings measured as real distances and gated on fleet strength. Unranked sovereign states use public order-of-magnitude estimates and are labelled est. wherever they appear. Borders, alliances and the idea that nations simply absorb one another are the game\u2019s, not the world\u2019s.
